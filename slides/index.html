Previous research

Memory content redistribution between coupled predictive-coding networks

Hypothesis: two predictive systems

  • Both systems predict aspects of the same world
  • Their internal representations differ
  • They agree when they make the same predictions about the world
  • When the fast learner is updated, their predictions can diverge
  • Offline (sleep), with few sensory inputs and motor outputs, each system can only predict the other system's spontaneous activity
Cheng 2024 · Rao & Ballard 1999 · Kok & Turk-Browne 2018 · Varga et al. 2025

Cheng (2024) proposes rapid hippocampal encoding of episodic traces and gradual neocortical learning of semantic information. Recall combines the trace with existing semantic knowledge.

Rao & Ballard (1999) proposed a hierarchical model of visual cortex. Feedback carries predictions of activity at lower levels, and feedforward signals carry the residual prediction errors.

Kok & Turk-Browne (2018) found that the hippocampus represented which shape an auditory cue predicted, even on trials where visual cortex primarily represented a different, presented shape.

In Varga et al. (2025), violations of a specific remembered event triggered hippocampal mismatch responses. Violations of familiar schemas engaged semantic control and multiple-demand networks. These studies motivate the model's comparison of predictions. The proposed divergence after a fast update is a model assumption.

Hypothesis: agreement is restored offline

  • Spontaneous reactivation could help both systems learn to predict each other. In our model, only the student learns during redistribution.
  • Similar to a form of distillation, during which semantic learning corresponds to the building of a predictive model of episodic traces
  • Following the predictive coding principle, mutual prediction errors constitute what neuronal populations communicate to each other
Semantic learning from replay · Cheng 2017, 2024

Cheng (2017) and Cheng (2024) propose that hippocampal replay can support semantic learning. In the biological analogy, agreement concerns regularities across episodes. The hippocampal trace still carries the sequence of the particular episode. Mutual learning from spontaneous activity is our hypothesis.

Hypothesis: low prediction error, same predictions of the world

Goal of this work

Show how neural networks can couple through prediction errors to bring their predictive models into agreement

Requirements for the model

  • Local activity and plasticity rules
  • Bio-plausible coupling
  • Fully autonomous
  • Easy to analyse mathematically
  • No fine-tuning required

A first, simplified setting: two associative memories

  • Each network is an associative memory
  • Disagreement is easy to define, and the maths stay clean
  • Both networks use the same representation, for now
  • The model, and especially the coupling modalities, should generalise to redistribution between models with different representations

Agreement between two linear associative memories

Their predictive models agree when both hold the same attractor manifold

Same memory subspace, same predictions of the world

  • Half of the units receive the sensory input, the other half predict the state of the world
  • With the same memory subspace, the same sensory input gives the same prediction of the state of the world

The units on the right are set by the sensory input, and recall fills in the units on the left. Recall settles on the memory subspace, so two networks that hold the same subspace fill in the same prediction for the same input.

Coupling principle

Goal: structure spontaneous activity so that the redistribution of the teacher's memory subspace is fast and exhaustive

Coupling
  • Bottom-up excitation: teacher → student
  • Top-down inhibition: student → teacher
Resulting dynamics
  1. The teacher settles on a point of its memory subspace
  2. If the student reconstructs it well, the student inhibits the teacher there
  3. The teacher drifts toward points the student reconstructs poorly
  4. The student learns from this trajectory

Classical PC vs ours: the sign of the feedback

Side arrows of the unit circuit = the two arrows of the network drawing: bottom-up drive (blue) and top-down influence (blue = positive in classical PC, red = negative in ours). Top left of each panel: the two networks, one neuron of each ringed (neuron i of the cortex, neuron i of the hippocampus) with the interface between them. Bottom right: that pair as units. Same three units, same synapses except one (highlighted): how the interface error acts on the teacher. Classical PC: descending the interface error w.r.t. x_T gives -pi_TS eps_TS, a negative influence of the error on the teacher; the student's influence on the error is negative too, so two negatives make a positive top-down influence. Ours: kappa is positive, so the student's negative influence on the error passes through as a negative top-down influence on the hippocampus, as in Shin and Jadhav. Negative influence is a sign, not silencing: it can raise some neurons' activity.

Previous research

Biological inspiration

Inspiration: top-down inhibition from the PFC

  • During sleep, independent PFC ripples suppress CA1 activity, potentially influencing replay
  • During wake, prefrontal–hippocampal synchrony via the nucleus reuniens supports extinction recall and reduces conditioned freezing
Shin & Jadhav 2024 (graphical abstract, cropped) · Malik et al. 2022 · Totty et al. 2023

Two routes for the PFC inhibition of CA1. Direct: long-range GABAergic PFC axons in CA1 (Malik et al. 2022). Through the nucleus reuniens (NR): the PFC projects densely to NR, and NR axons reach CA1. They are glutamatergic, so the inhibition of CA1 pyramidal cells is polysynaptic, through CA1 interneurons (Goswamee et al. 2021).

In awake rats, NR supports prefrontal–hippocampal theta synchrony and the recall of extinction, which reduces conditioned freezing (Totty et al. 2023).

CA1 → PFC is direct and one-way, mainly from ventral and intermediate CA1. The PFC also reaches EC (lateral EC). The rest of the neocortex (faded) exchanges with the hippocampus mainly through EC.

Not bio-realistic: the network architecture

  • The architecture is not realistic: neuron-to-neuron coupling through dedicated interface error units
  • Cortical activity can bias the content of hippocampal replay. We propose that the PFC can veto replay in the EC → CA3 → CA1 loop
  • If the PFC vetoes what it already predicts, the model shows that this alone could steer replay toward the episodic traces the PFC predicts worst: the most novel ones
  • Novelty for the PFC can take several forms
    • a newly encoded episode
    • a trace not yet consolidated
    • a trace whose associated reward has changed
Shin & Jadhav 2024 · Rothschild et al. 2017 · Giri et al. 2019 · Schapiro et al. 2018 · Ambrose et al. 2016

The architecture is not meant to be realistic: one interface error unit per neuron pair, coupled one to one, same code in both networks.

Cortical activity can bias the content of hippocampal replay. Rothschild, Eban & Frank (2017) found that auditory cortex activity before ripples predicted the content of hippocampal replay. In the proposed circuit, replay runs through the EC → CA3 → CA1 loop, closed either within the hippocampus (CA1 back to EC) or through the PFC (CA1 → PFC → EC). The PFC can suppress it: Shin & Jadhav (2024) found that independent PFC ripples suppress CA1 reactivation during sleep. That the PFC suppresses precisely what it already predicts is my hypothesis.

Anatomy. CA1 → PFC is a direct, one-way projection, mainly from ventral and intermediate CA1 and the subiculum. There is no major direct excitatory projection from the PFC back to CA1. The PFC reaches the hippocampus through the lateral entorhinal cortex and the nucleus reuniens, and through a direct long-range GABAergic projection to CA1 (Malik et al. 2022). The veto is drawn on CA1 by its two candidate routes: direct, and through the nucleus reuniens (NR). The PFC projects densely to NR, and NR axons reach CA1 (not CA3). They are glutamatergic: they excite CA1 pyramidal cells and interneurons, and the inhibition of pyramidal cells is polysynaptic, through the interneurons (Goswamee et al. 2021). NR also supports extinction recall and the resulting reduction in conditioned freezing (Totty et al. 2023). The rest of the neocortex exchanges with the hippocampus mainly through the entorhinal cortex; CA1 also projects directly to a few cortical areas, e.g. retrosplenial cortex.

The model implements a graded version of this veto: inhibition in proportion to what the student reconstructs. The theorem shows that this alone makes replay climb the student's surprise, with no replay scheduler and no explicit evaluation of each memory's utility.

Under this reading, replay would favour any trace the PFC mispredicts, whatever the reason. New episode: reactivation lasts for hours after a novel experience (Giri et al. 2019). Weakly learned trace: Schapiro et al. (2018). Changed reward: reverse replay rate follows changes in reward (Ambrose, Pfeiffer & Foster 2016).

Caution: reward is not in the model, so the reward case is a prediction of the hypothesis, not a simulation result. In Ambrose et al., reverse replay rises when reward increases but falls when it decreases; a pure mismatch account would predict a rise in both cases.

Not bio-realistic: the neural substrate

  • The model is rate-based: no spikes, no sharp-wave ripples
  • It reactivates static patterns, whereas the hippocampus replays sequences during ripples
  • Missing: an implementation that tests whether the neural substrate can produce the dynamics the model obtains with predictive coding
  • What would be nice: an implementation in a spiking neural network, with actual sharp-wave ripples and sequential replay
Buzsáki 2015 (Fig. 3A) · Ehsani & Cheng 2026

In rodents, hippocampal memory reactivation happens mostly during sharp-wave ripples: bursts of fast oscillation in CA1 (roughly 150–250 Hz), lasting about 30–100 ms, during slow-wave sleep and quiet wakefulness. The sharp wave proper is a negative deflection in stratum radiatum, driven by CA3; the ripple is recorded in the pyramidal layer. During a ripple, place cells fire in sequences, forward or reverse, about 10–20 times faster than during behaviour (Buzsáki 2015). The figure is Buzsáki (2015), Fig. 3A, rat CA1: left, an isolated sharp-wave ripple; right, a burst of three. Top, pyramidal layer (wideband); middle, the same signal filtered at 50–250 Hz, which isolates the ripple; bottom, stratum radiatum, where the sharp wave is the large negative deflection. The model has none of this: rate units, no oscillations, and reactivation is a noisy drift of a rate vector over the memory subspace.

The model shows what the dynamics should do; it does not show that neurons can do it. The open question is whether a spiking network produces the same priority rule: prediction errors at the interface, a PFC veto implemented by realistic inhibition or disinhibition (e.g. through CA1 interneurons), and replay that moves away from what the cortex already predicts.

Possible starting point: Ehsani & Cheng (2026), a spiking place-cell network in which sequences are generated by background noise and the balance of excitation and inhibition, with offline replay during spontaneous population bursts. Their diffusive replay mode, which resembles a random walk, is the closest analogue of the teacher's noisy drift. Prediction errors: inhibitory plasticity can learn to cancel predicted input while keeping responses to mismatches (Hertäg & Sprekeler 2020).

No semantic learning in the current model

  • Both networks store the same traces, in the same code
  • Redistribution is a transfer: the student copies the teacher's memory subspace
  • Missing: semantic learning, the extraction of regularities shared across episodes, which replay is thought to drive in the neocortex
Cheng 2017, 2024 · McClelland, McNaughton & O'Reilly 1995

Both networks use the same code and are coupled one to one, so the student's learning reproduces the teacher's memory subspace: redistribution is a transfer of traces. In both rows of the drawing, the arrow is what replay achieves: a copy in the model, semantic learning in the account below.

In the semantic learning account (Cheng 2017, Cheng 2024), replay of episodic traces lets the neocortex extract statistical regularities across experiences, rather than reproduce the traces. Consolidation is then a by-product of semantic learning. Episodic recall stays generative: it combines a hippocampal trace with semantic knowledge (scenario construction; Cheng, Werning & Suddendorf 2016).

So the model covers the transfer view of systems consolidation, not the construction of semantic representations. Next step: different codes in the two networks, with the mapping between them learned through plasticity, to test whether feedback-driven prioritisation still speeds up cortical learning of the regularities shared by hippocampal traces.

For questions

Keeping the teacher on its memories

Redistribution needs $x_T$ near its memory subspace $\mathcal U_T$
$$\cK \;<\; \pi_T\,\sigma^2_{\min}$$
$\sigma^2_{\min}$: smallest nonzero eigenvalue of $M_T^\top M_T$
  • Off $\mathcal U_T$, the recurrent error pulls $x_T$ back at rate $\ge \pi_T\sigma^2_{\min}$
  • $\cK\,\varepsilon_{TS}$ contains $+\cK\,x_T$: a push away at rate $\cK$
  • $\cK$ too large: the teacher leaves its memories
A heuristic: it favours confinement, it does not guarantee it.
Teacher activity is reactivation only while it sits on (or near) its memory subspace U_T = ker M_T. Off U_T the recurrent term -pi_T M_T^T M_T x_T restores it, slowest along the smallest nonzero singular direction: rate pi_T sigma_min^2. The interface term kappa (x_T - x_S) has a +kappa x_T part that pushes x_T outward in every direction at rate kappa. Net restoring rate pi_T sigma_min^2 - kappa must stay positive. Heuristic: it ignores the -kappa x_S part and the noise.

Result only, proof in the paper (Theorem 1). With the cortex settled, the mismatch left is N_S x_T, and it is exactly the gradient of q: the interface drive on the hippocampus points uphill. Diagram: a 2-D cut of the memory sphere through one learned direction (q low, grey) and one not yet learned (q high, green). The settled cortex reproduces the learned part and little of the rest, so the mismatch, and the drive, point toward the unlearned direction. The norm constraint keeps the tangential part: the state slides along the circle toward the green maxima.

So replay should track $\mathcal F_{\max}$

  • Feedback climbs $q$, noise keeps replay exploring
  • The teacher should settle at a maximum of $q$ on its memory subspace
  • One of the states $x_T^*$ where $q = \mathcal F_{\max}$
The theorem gives the direction, not convergence: we test it next.
If the drive is gradient ascent on q along the memory sphere, the hippocampus should end up near a maximum: a state with q = F_max, one of the maximisers (at least the two signs of the worst direction). There the drive is purely radial (the mismatch points straight out), the norm constraint absorbs it and the climb stops. Not proved: noise and the norm constraint mean the theorem gives a direction, not convergence. Next slide tests it.

Building block: inference and learning

Prediction error and free energy
$$\varepsilon = x - W x \qquad F = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\,\lVert\varepsilon\rVert^2$$
Fast inference on activity
$$\tau\,\dot x = -\nabla_x F = -\pi\,(I-W)^\top \varepsilon$$
Slow learning on weights (local, Hebbian)
$$\dot W_{ij} = \eta\,\pi\;\varepsilon_i x_j\quad (i\neq j),\qquad W_{ii}=0$$
Both dynamics descend the same free energy: activity fast, weights slow. The learning rule only uses the local error and presynaptic activity. Self-connections stay fixed at zero. We set the bias terms in Tang's circuit to zero. The same equations apply to the hippocampus (teacher) and the cortex (student).

Memories are zero-error states

After learning, every stored memory $m$ is predicted perfectly
$$(I-W)\,m = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F(m) = 0$$
  • Inference from a partial cue descends $F$: the memory is completed
  • Linear network: any mix of memories also has $F=0$, a flat memory subspace
$$\mathcal U = \ker(I-W)$$
Tang et al. 2023 (Fig. 4A, implicit covPCN, 64 MNIST images stored)
The bottom half of the digit is masked and the network fills it in by running inference. In the linear case the stored content is a subspace, not isolated point attractors as in Hopfield networks: this subspace is what the hippocampus will reactivate.

The advantage grows with correlation

Fixed corpus, only the within-group correlation changes. Replay is the same at every rho: the groups span the same subspace, so the hippocampal dynamics do not change. Uniform rehearsal slows down as rho grows: random memories of a group give more and more overlapping updates. Uncorrelated memories: rehearsal wins, each update is an exact memory. At 0.35 they tie, at 0.70 replay wins.

The student alone can now recall the memories

A: at each time, freeze the student, set it to a full digit and measure how well it predicts it; 100% = empty student. Each digit drops when its directions are transferred. B: clamp half of the neurons to a digit, let the rest settle, no coupling and no learning. At the partial checkpoint the student completes some digits, at the late one all three, close to the teacher.
