Saighi · Memory transfer

Memory transfer between coupled predictive-coding networks

Paul Saighi
Part 1

Motivation

Where this started: continual learning

Learn new memories without forgetting the old ones

Step 1: transfer memories from one network to another
Link with the talk I gave to the lab in December. The plan for continual learning: new memories go to a fast store, old ones sit in a long-term store, and both are transferred into a synthesis network that ends up holding old and new together (the predictive-rehearsal idea). Both arrows are memory transfers, so transfer is the first brick of the plan.

Building a framework for memory transfer

  • I needed a mathematically grounded model of memory transfer
  • Grounded in predictive coding
    • links normative and mechanistic views
    • error correction could drive transfer autonomously
What I realised: there is little bio-plausible, mathematically grounded theory of how one network transfers its memories to another, and that is the first step of the plan. So this talk is about that step. I ground it in predictive coding: it links the normative view (inference, free energy) and the mechanistic one (local error units, local plasticity), and I suspected its error-correction dynamics could drive the transfer on their own, without an external teacher signal. The rest of the talk tests that.

Sleep reactivation transfers memories

  • During sleep, the hippocampus reactivates recent experience
  • Reactivation is coordinated with the neocortex
  • Systems consolidation: the neocortex gradually becomes the main actor of memory recall*
Wilson & McNaughton 1994 · McClelland et al. 1995 · Klinzing et al. 2019
Reactivation = intrinsic activity replaying waking patterns. Systems consolidation read as memory transfer: the neocortex acquires content first stored in the hippocampus.

Reactivation is selective

  • Recent experience is reactivated most, then fades
Mehta 2007 (schematic) · Kudrimoti, Barnes & McNaughton 1999 (Fig. 3D)
Reactivation = how much of the running co-firing pattern of hippocampal cell pairs reappears in the sleep that follows (explained variance; PRE bar = pattern already there before running). Strongest in the first 10 min, decays with a time constant of about 30 min.

Reactivation is selective

  • Repeated experience is reactivated less
How does the brain pick what to replay?
Giri et al. 2019 (Fig. 5)
One rat, same environment on three consecutive days; 3 h of sleep after each session. Black = reactivation (explained variance: how much of the running co-firing structure reappears in POST sleep, beyond what PRE sleep already had). Green = control (reversed explained variance, PRE and POST swapped), i.e. chance level. Strong for hours on day 1, weaker on day 2, at chance on day 3. Mechanism of this prioritisation is unknown.

A CA1 ↔ PFC motif during sleep

  • CA1 activity recruits PFC
  • Independent PFC ripples suppress CA1
  • The most suppressed assemblies are those reactivated with PFC
Wierzynski et al. 2009 · Shin & Jadhav 2024 (graphical abstract)
Asymmetric and selective: hippocampus drives specific prefrontal populations, prefrontal reactivation suppresses the matching hippocampal representation.

Hypothesis: cortical feedback steers replay

  • Hippocampus drives cortical learning
  • Cortex silences what it already knows
  • Replay drifts to what is not yet transferred
Here: selective feedback, in predictive coding
Wang, Poe & Zochowski 2008 (diffuse cortical feedback)
Wang et al.: cortical feedback onto hippocampal interneurons, effective once the cortical trace has matured, so consolidated traces stop being replayed. We extend it: predictive coding, selective rather than diffuse negative feedback, and show it gives fast transfer of correlated memories.
Part 2

Model

Two coupled memories: teacher and student

Same building block twice. Hippocampus = teacher T, already holds the memories, weights fixed during transfer. Prefrontal cortex = student S, learns. Bottom-up drive from T to S, prediction error back from S to T: the CA1-PFC motif of the motivation.

Building block: a predictive-coding memory

Hippocampus and cortex: each a covPCN, a predictive-coding network that stores memories

Tang et al. 2023, PLOS Comput Biol (Fig. 1B, implicit covPCN), linear version. Both networks of the model are the same circuit: the implicit covariance-learning predictive-coding network (covPCN) of Tang et al. Value neurons x, one error neuron per value neuron. Each error neuron compares its neuron with the prediction from the others. Next slide: its equations.

Building block: inference and learning

Prediction error and free energy
$$\varepsilon = x - W x \qquad F = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\,\lVert\varepsilon\rVert^2$$
Fast inference on activity
$$\tau\,\dot x = -\nabla_x F = -\pi\,(I-W)^\top \varepsilon$$
Slow learning on weights (local, Hebbian)
$$\dot W = \eta\,\pi\;\varepsilon\,x^\top$$
Both dynamics descend the same free energy: activity fast, weights slow. The learning rule only uses the local error and presynaptic activity. Same equations for the hippocampus (teacher) and the cortex (student).

Memories are zero-error states

After learning, every stored memory $m$ is predicted perfectly
$$(I-W)\,m = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F(m) = 0$$
  • Inference from a partial cue descends $F$: the memory is completed
  • Linear network: any mix of memories also has $F=0$, a flat memory subspace
$$\mathcal U = \ker(I-W)$$
Tang et al. 2023 (Fig. 4A, implicit covPCN, 64 MNIST images stored)
The bottom half of the digit is masked and the network fills it in by running inference. In the linear case the stored content is a subspace, not isolated point attractors as in Hopfield networks: this subspace is what the hippocampus will reactivate.

Classical PC vs ours: the sign of the feedback

Side arrows of the unit circuit = the two arrows of the network drawing: bottom-up drive (blue) and top-down influence (blue = positive in classical PC, red = negative in ours). Top left of each panel: the two networks, one neuron of each ringed (neuron i of the cortex, neuron i of the hippocampus) with the interface between them. Bottom right: that pair as units. Same three units, same synapses except one (highlighted): how the interface error acts on the teacher. Classical PC: descending the interface error w.r.t. x_T gives -pi_TS eps_TS, a negative influence of the error on the teacher; the student's influence on the error is negative too, so two negatives make a positive top-down influence. Ours: kappa is positive, so the student's negative influence on the error passes through as a negative top-down influence on the hippocampus, as in Shin and Jadhav. Negative influence is a sign, not silencing: it can raise some neurons' activity.

Why reverse the sign: a negative cortical influence

Classical PC
Our model
positive influence
negative influence, as observed
Wierzynski et al. 2009 · Shin & Jadhav 2024
Back to the biology of the motivation. In classical predictive coding the top-down influence on the lower network is positive: the cortex would pull the hippocampus toward what it already predicts. During sleep the PFC does the opposite to CA1: its independent ripples suppress CA1, most of all the assemblies it reactivated with. Reversing the sign of the interface synapse gives a negative influence, in line with that observation.

Coupling through an interface error

Two neurons per network shown. Each network is a covPCN: value units x, error units eps, recurrent weights W through the error units. Neuron i of the teacher and neuron i of the student encode the same component (shared code, one-to-one pathway, simplification) and meet at one interface error unit eps_TS,i.

Coupling through an interface error

Each network $\alpha \in \{\cS{S}, \cT{T}\}$ as before, with $M_\alpha = I - W_\alpha$, $\;\varepsilon_\alpha = M_\alpha x_\alpha$
One interface error unit per neuron pair
$$\varepsilon_{TS} = \cT{x_T} - \cS{x_S}$$
Student: conventional PC, descends its free energy
$$\tau_S\,\dot x_S = -\pi_S M_S^\top \varepsilon_S + \pi_{TS}\,\varepsilon_{TS}$$
Teacher: sign reversed, $\cK > 0$
$$\tau_T\,\dot x_T = -\pi_T M_T^\top \varepsilon_T + \cK\,\varepsilon_{TS} + \sigma_\xi\,\xi_T, \quad \lVert x_T\rVert = 1$$
$\cS{x_S} \to \varepsilon_{TS} \to \cT{x_T}$ is negative: the cortex pushes the hippocampus away from what it already predicts
Left: the diagram of the previous slide, to keep the units in view. Student side is standard PC: the interface error has a positive influence on it, toward the teacher. Teacher side: in standard PC the error would push x_T toward x_S; here kappa is positive, so the more the student already reproduces x_T, the less drive the teacher gets. Noise keeps the teacher exploring, unit norm idealises gain control.

The coupled dynamics

$$\begin{aligned} \textcolor{#5F6670}{\textsf{student}}\qquad \tau_S\,\dot x_S &= -\pi_S M_S^\top \varepsilon_S \;+\; \pi_{TS}\,\varepsilon_{TS} \\[1.2em] \textcolor{#5F6670}{\textsf{teacher}}\qquad \tau_T\,\dot x_T &= -\pi_T M_T^\top \varepsilon_T \;+\; \cK\,\varepsilon_{TS} \;+\; \sigma_\xi\,\xi_T, \qquad \lVert x_T\rVert = 1 \end{aligned}$$
$M_\alpha = I - W_\alpha$
$\alpha \in \{S, T\}$
$\varepsilon_S = M_S\,x_S$
cortex prediction error
$\varepsilon_T = M_T\,x_T$
hippocampus prediction error
$\varepsilon_{TS} = x_T - x_S$
interface error
classical PC would have  $-\pi_{TS}\,\varepsilon_{TS}$  on the teacher
Both networks descend their own recurrent free energy. The student term is the usual PC gradient of the interface error. The teacher term has the opposite sign (kappa > 0): this is the one place where the model leaves predictive coding, and it is what turns the student's feedback into a negative top-down influence.

Two phases: storage, then transfer

  • A  Teacher clamped to the memories, learns $W_T$
  • B  Clamp off, interface on: intrinsic activity only
  • $W_T$ frozen, only $W_S$ learns
  • Student never sees the original data
A: interleaved clamping (no pattern separation in the model). B: teacher reactivates from noise inside its memory subspace; kappa kept below pi_T sigma_min^2 so the drive does not push it off the subspace (supplementary slide).

Results

Maps: the same path coloured by how fast the hippocampus moves (top) and by the drive (bottom): the fast jumps to new directions are where the drive is high; while a mode is learned in place the drive is low and the state jitters. How fast the cortex's weights change along the path: fast learning on the jumps to directions it does not yet reproduce, slow once they are learned. Local rule: its own prediction error times its activity.

The cortex alone can now recall the memories

A: at each time, freeze the student, set it to a full digit and measure how well it predicts it; 100% = empty student. Each digit drops when its directions are transferred. B: clamp half of the neurons to a digit, let the rest settle, no coupling and no learning. At the partial checkpoint the student completes some digits, at the late one all three, close to the teacher.

New memories renew reactivation and transfer

One run, 6 random memories. The hippocampus starts with 3, learns memory 4 at t = 9k, memories 5 and 6 at t = 16k; the cortex is never reset. A: modes already transferred stay low; each new memory opens a new gap that then closes. B: how much each memory is present in hippocampal activity. Right after it is stored, the new memory dominates replay: replay goes where the cortex is missing something.

Correlated memories: two ways to teach the cortex

The memories come in groups of similar patterns: cosine 0.8 inside a group, orthogonal between groups. The hippocampus acquires one group after another during transfer (groups of 8, 7, 6, 5 memories, added at 57k, 125k, 188k events), without resetting the cortex. Two ways to teach the cortex. Ours: replay in the coupled network, the cortex only sees hippocampal activity. Control: no hippocampus, the cortex is trained directly on the memories active at that time (the groups acquired so far); at every update one memory, sampled uniformly, is clamped onto it (non-prioritised replay). Same rule, same rate, same number of weight updates: the only difference is which activity drives learning.

Replay transfers correlated memories faster

Left: similarity of the 26 memories, four groups, 0.8 inside a group. Right: how much of the hippocampal memory subspace the cortex still misses, over the memories stored so far. Each new group makes it jump. Replay (violet) closes every gap much faster than uniform rehearsal (grey): rehearsal keeps reinforcing the shared direction of the group it samples, replay moves on as soon as the cortex reproduces it, so effort follows what is missing, not how many memories a group has. End: 0.03 vs 0.30.

The advantage grows with correlation

Fixed corpus, only the within-group correlation changes. Replay is the same at every rho: the groups span the same subspace, so the hippocampal dynamics do not change. Uniform rehearsal slows down as rho grows: random memories of a group give more and more overlapping updates. Uncorrelated memories: rehearsal wins, each update is an exact memory. At 0.35 they tie, at 0.70 replay wins.
Same run as the first results slides, the whole path at once, now with the stored digits (both signs). Replay rarely sits on a stored memory: most of the time it is a mixture of them. So the hippocampus is not cycling through its memories. It follows something else, which changes as the cortex learns. That is the question for the next slides.

Replay climbs the cortex's surprise

Proved: with a fast-settling cortex, the interface drive pushes the hippocampal state up the gradient of the cortex's surprise
the cortex's surprise at a replayed pattern $x_T$: its free energy after settling
$$q(x_T) \;=\; \min_{x_S}\, F_S(x_S, x_T)$$
Hypothesis: replay therefore tracks $\mathcal F_{\max}$, the hippocampal memory state that surprises the cortex most
$$\mathcal F_{\max} \;=\; \max_{x_T \in \mathbb S_T}\, q(x_T)$$
$\mathbb S_T$: unit-norm states of the hippocampal memory subspace
Result first. q(x_T): replay a pattern, let the cortex settle (it is fast), the free energy left is the cortex's surprise at that pattern; zero on what it has learned. Theorem 1 (paper, and Supplementary): if the cortex settles fast (tau_S much smaller than tau_T, learning slower still), the interface drive on the hippocampus is exactly (kappa / pi_TS) times the gradient of q. So the drive always points uphill in the cortex's surprise; on the unit sphere only its tangential part moves the state. Diagram: one learned direction (horizontal, q low) and one not yet learned (vertical, q high). The theorem gives the direction, not where replay ends up: hypothesis, replay tracks the maxima of q on the memory sphere, F_max. Tested on the next slide.
The test. Five MNIST digits stored one at a time; three "next" per memory. 1: the memory arrives, green = high free energy for the cortex, the green ring is the F_max state. 2: cortical plasticity off (amber in the plot), replay climbs to the ring and stays, F_max flat. 3: plasticity on, the cortex learns it, F_max falls, the dot turns grey and the ring fades; replay then wanders. Map: 2-D layout of the hippocampal memory sphere, distances approximate.
Summary

Take-home

Conclusion

  • Two coupled predictive-coding networks, local rules only: replay transfers the memories to the cortex
  • Cortical feedback steers replay toward what the cortex has not learned yet
  • New memories are replayed first, transfer resumes when memories are added
  • Correlated memories: faster than uniform rehearsal
  • Replay tracks the pattern the cortex explains worst, $\mathcal F_{\max}$
Replay priority emerges from the mismatch between hippocampus and cortex
One line per result. The architecture: two predictive-coding memories, hippocampus and cortex, coupled through an interface error, local plasticity only. Replay transfers the memories mode by mode, and the cortex can then recall them alone. The cortical feedback is what selects: it pushes replay toward what the cortex does not reproduce yet (theorem: gradient ascent on q). New memories open a new gap and dominate replay. On correlated memories this beats uniform rehearsal, more so as correlation grows. Replay follows F_max. Message: priority is not a property of the hippocampal trace (recency, strength), it comes from the relation between the two networks.

Limitations

  • Linear networks
  • Same neural code in both networks, coupled one to one
    • hippocampal indexing theory: the hippocampus stores an index, not a copy
  • Direct coupling: no entorhinal relay, no polysynaptic routes
  • Memories copied as they are: no abstraction or schematisation
  • No reward or neuromodulation in the choice of what to replay
  • Tracking of $\mathcal F_{\max}$ shown in simulation: the theorem gives the direction, not convergence
All in the paper's discussion. Linear units make the memory content a subspace and the analysis exact, but omit nonlinear responses and spiking. One-to-one correspondence between hippocampal and cortical neurons: indexing theory says the hippocampus holds an index linked to cortical patterns, with its own sparse code; our model is the limiting case where both projections are fixed and one to one. Anatomy goes through the entorhinal cortex, plus CA1 to PFC and the nucleus reuniens. Consolidation also transforms memories (gist, schemas); here the student acquires exactly what the teacher stores. Reward is known to bias reactivation. Theory: the theorem is local (the drive points uphill), convergence to F_max is a numerical result.

Mathematics: better understanding of the coupled dynamics, for example convergence to F_max. Bio-plausibility: mainly nonlinear activation functions (and, further, separate codes linked by learned projections). Sequences: hippocampal replay is sequential, transfer trajectories rather than fixed patterns. Continual learning: back to the plan of the beginning, transfer of new and old memories into a synthesis network. Machine learning: the same dynamics in standard architectures, as a form of offline, autonomous distillation from one model to another. Active inference: the hippocampus climbs toward states of high neocortical free energy, i.e. it samples where the cortex is most surprised. This resembles epistemic, information-seeking behaviour in active inference, so replay selection could be cast as an internal action that minimises expected free energy.
Supplementary

For questions

Keeping the teacher on its memories

Transfer needs $x_T$ near its memory subspace $\mathcal U_T$
$$\cK \;<\; \pi_T\,\sigma^2_{\min}$$
$\sigma^2_{\min}$: smallest nonzero eigenvalue of $M_T^\top M_T$
  • Off $\mathcal U_T$, the recurrent error pulls $x_T$ back at rate $\ge \pi_T\sigma^2_{\min}$
  • $\cK\,\varepsilon_{TS}$ contains $+\cK\,x_T$: a push away at rate $\cK$
  • $\cK$ too large: the teacher leaves its memories
A heuristic: it favours confinement, it does not guarantee it.
Teacher activity is reactivation only while it sits on (or near) its memory subspace U_T = ker M_T. Off U_T the recurrent term -pi_T M_T^T M_T x_T restores it, slowest along the smallest nonzero singular direction: rate pi_T sigma_min^2. The interface term kappa (x_T - x_S) has a +kappa x_T part that pushes x_T outward in every direction at rate kappa. Net restoring rate pi_T sigma_min^2 - kappa must stay positive. Heuristic: it ignores the -kappa x_S part and the noise.

Theorem: the cortical feedback climbs $q$

if the cortex settles fast  $\tau_S \ll \tau_T \ll \eta_S^{-1}$
settled cortex
$$x_S^* \;=\; (I - N_S)\, x_T$$
settled free energy
$$q(x_T) \;=\; \tfrac{\pi_{TS}}{2}\; x_T^\top N_S\, x_T$$
drive on the hippocampus
$$\cK\,(x_T - x_S^*) \;=\; \frac{\cK}{\pi_{TS}}\, \nabla_{x_T}\, q$$
$N_S$: zero on what the cortex has learned, positive on what it has not
the feedback is gradient ascent on $q$
Result only, proof in the paper (Theorem 1). With the cortex settled, the mismatch left is N_S x_T, and it is exactly the gradient of q: the interface drive on the hippocampus points uphill. Diagram: a 2-D cut of the memory sphere through one learned direction (q low, grey) and one not yet learned (q high, green). The settled cortex reproduces the learned part and little of the rest, so the mismatch, and the drive, point toward the unlearned direction. The norm constraint keeps the tangential part: the state slides along the circle toward the green maxima.

So replay should track $\mathcal F_{\max}$

  • Feedback climbs $q$, noise keeps replay exploring
  • The hippocampus should settle at a maximum of $q$ on its memory sphere
  • One of the states where $q = \mathcal F_{\max}$
The theorem gives the direction, not convergence: we test it next.
If the drive is gradient ascent on q along the memory sphere, the hippocampus should end up near a maximum: a state with q = F_max, one of the maximisers (at least the two signs of the worst direction). There the drive is purely radial (the mismatch points straight out), the norm constraint absorbs it and the climb stops. Not proved: noise and the norm constraint mean the theorem gives a direction, not convergence. Next slide tests it.
